# After the first aging ladder

The source-bound ladder completed on the held volume. The correct reading is 59, 44 and 30 completed and adopted index jobs, with sustained rates of 15,242.78, 16,330.62 and 18,081.59 indexed records/s. The original certificate stays intact. The corrected checker requires its exact digest, outer custody inventory, original source and attempt, all original artifacts, the matching transactor PID and one physical database identity across the three arms. On the server it also checks the same native catalog ID, held checkpoint and live cut before the first continuation write.

## What limited throughput

CPU usage was 2.66–2.71 of three cores. Completed index job durations sum to 688, 749 and 762 seconds in 828–836 second windows. These are about 83%, 90% and 91% of each arm. Apply summed to 724, 799 and 814 seconds (44, 56 and 54 microseconds per indexed record). The per-transaction observations overlap and cannot be subtracted from wall time. WAL sync means were 0.170, 0.266 and 0.296 ms. The WAL sync times summed to 219, 188 and 199 seconds, including indexing. There were no back-pressure alarms, and the memory index stayed under 458 MiB against a 2 GiB cap. Larger groups cut physical transaction counts from 818,218 to 70,275 and 25,112, but the work to apply and index each record remained. Saturated CPU and concurrent index work are the strongest supported bottleneck. The trace leaves I/O stalls inside individual jobs possible.

The old arm changed five settings and available CPU together. The evidence cannot identify the largest individual effect among the three-core allowance, object cache, heap, index threshold and parallelism. WAL stayed durable and was fast in the new arms. Large memory-index caps did not come into play during the ladder. The new continuation spends more CPU on the proven solo layout, caps indexing at four workers on six physical cores, and raises the threshold from 128 MiB to 256 MiB to reduce frequent segment replacement. It keeps the 24 GiB heap, 16 GiB object cache, 2 GiB memory-index maximum and write concurrency of four. Pin PostgreSQL separately. Keep synchronous commit, fsync and full-page writes on. [Datomic index and import guidance](https://docs.datomic.com/operation/capacity.html#indexing)

**Next week.** The full-size solo native loader delivered 762–988 saves/s with 256 in flight. The 346–365 product writer result therefore has headroom somewhere before or at native submission under a similar profile. The comparisons do not isolate SDK/gateway processing, per-person ordering and offer concurrency, the extra product transaction-function work or read contention. A matched tape with timestamps at gateway entry, native enqueue and durable reply would locate that gap.

## Before-run predictions and stop rules

Use solo K at depth 256. The 24-member arm was faster by sustained indexed count but added 0.91 GB per million against solo's 0.71 and changed index geometry. This continuation retains the already-written grouped spans. Predict 20–30k indexed records/s during writing and **18–26k/s including collection, vacuum and index adoption**. From 477,456,017 that implies **5.58–8.06 loading hours**, or **$6.08–$8.79** remaining. Including the completed 44m47s ladder gives **6.33–8.81 hours and $6.90–$9.60**. Start with 20 minutes of writing, then measure the combined record advance through the first maintenance cycle. Stop if the combined rate falls below 9,900/s or no longer fits 16 loading hours including the ladder. Keep sampling across later cycles. The raw events still distinguish active write throughput and index work from maintenance time. A rate near the old ladder despite six busy cores and a settled index would falsify the expected scaling. A rate near the ladder with spare CPU redirects attention to index/SQL waits or the loader.

## Disk growth and the maintained counterfactual

In the archived minute buckets the three arms logged 420,344, 490,692 and 572,211 index segment writes. Live index inventory went from 1,863,733 to 2,005,182 segments across the ladder, a net increase of 141,449. That leaves roughly **1.34 million extra segment writes** beyond net growth. The buckets miss the arm edges and do not include segment byte lengths. About 38 GB was added to PostgreSQL allocation. The archive has neither a live/obsolete byte census nor PostgreSQL dead-tuple and free-space-map measurements, so it cannot split that allocation exactly into replaced Datomic segments, PostgreSQL dead/free heap and TOAST space, native log objects and new live index data. The three arms generated **37.06 GB of WAL**. Only **1.66 GB** was retained alongside the stopped database. Generated WAL is I/O, not permanent disk growth. Installing the group schema added **18 indexed datoms**. Recurring group refs, position keys and retirement markers change live amplification and leaf packing. The measured per-record increase for grouped arms is consistent with that change and with increasing index replacement. The archive cannot separate the two.

The continuation uses Datomic's supported `datomic.tools.gc-db` after the held cut, after the initial 20-minute write window, after each 30-minute write window, and after the final window. The writer drains, verifies its indexed census and exits before collection. During this private initial import a cutoff two wall-clock minutes before collection leaves a trailing margin, and all other readers and backups must be closed. The new writer starts on the latest fully indexed cut. Ordinary PostgreSQL `VACUUM (INDEX_CLEANUP ON, TRUNCATE OFF, PARALLEL 2)` makes deleted heap, TOAST and index pages reusable. It generally holds the allocated footprint rather than shrinking existing files. This vacuum leaves the existing heap in place. Each cycle logs allocated PGDATA (including WAL), SQL heap/TOAST/index and WAL sizes, segments collected, and deleted and dead-tuple counters. Compare post-vacuum allocation after the following write to see whether space was reused. The runner stops on a physical or filesystem floor and if the cumulative and latest-cycle projections after two completed write-and-maintenance cycles both exceed the approved ceiling. [Datomic storage collection](https://docs.datomic.com/operation/capacity.html#garbage-collection-for-live-databases) · [PostgreSQL vacuum](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/routine-vacuuming.html)

**Before-run size prediction: 330–390 GB allocated at 1B, with 400 GB as the primary limit.** This is a planning range. It carries forward the roughly 125 GB baseline, assumes about 0.30–0.37 GB per million of additional records after allowing room for recurring group overhead in the existing spans, and allows tens of GB for churn between collections, reusable pages and WAL. The measured 0.71–0.91 GB/M without collection would project 534–639 GB from the held cut. If collection fails to flatten allocation, the job preserves the last verified prefix instead of growing into the remaining free space. There is a separately admitted 450 GB ceiling when the full two-device placement below has been secured. We have no measured live 1B directory size yet.

Collection removes retired physical segments with no current references. Current indexed history, newly opened as-of views, receipts and the sealed cut are checked before and after. Routine production collection is a closer match for an aged size than keeping every replaced segment. It removes the ability to rely on an indefinitely held physical database value backed by collected segments. The exclusively owned importer has no such readers. GC and vacuum are charged to loading and finish before the measurement transactor restarts. Their presence affects allocation, backup footprint and ordinary cache history. There is no deliberate cache prewarm.
